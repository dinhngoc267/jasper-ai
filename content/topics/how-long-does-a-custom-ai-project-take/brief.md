# Post Brief

**Title**: How Long a Custom AI Project Actually Takes — And the One Phase Most Timelines Skip

**Target keyword**: how long does it take to build a custom AI agent

**Audience**: Founders and technical leads at small-to-mid-size companies (2–250 people) evaluating a custom AI agent, RAG system, or LLM application, who need a realistic timeline to plan around — a launch date, a budget cycle, or a board update. Bottom-funnel: they're past "should we do this" and into "how do we plan for it."

**Angle**: Most timeline estimates for AI projects are really just software-project timelines with an "AI" label, and they fail for a specific, avoidable reason: they schedule "build the feature" before anyone has confirmed the chosen architecture actually works on the client's real data. A defensible timeline has two phases with different risk profiles — a short, bounded discovery/validation phase (days, not weeks) that either confirms or kills the architecture choice, followed by a fixed-scope build phase that can actually hold a date because the biggest unknown is already resolved. Vendors who quote one flat timeline for both are guessing on the part that's hardest to guess.

**Supporting points**:
- Lay out a realistic phase breakdown: discovery/architecture validation (test against real data, confirm fit) → fixed-scope build → integration/testing → launch — with rough relative durations, not hard promises.
- Explain why skipping discovery doesn't make projects faster — it just moves the uncertainty into the build phase, where it shows up as missed deadlines instead of a scoped decision point.
- Name the timeline killers specific to AI (not generic software): messy or inaccessible source data, unclear success criteria for "is the answer good enough," and scope creep from stakeholders discovering what's possible only after seeing a working demo.
- Give the reader a way to sanity-check any vendor's timeline: ask what happens to the schedule if the first architecture choice doesn't work — a vendor with no answer hasn't actually planned for AI's real risk profile.
- Reinforce the solo-engineer advantage: one person owns discovery and build, so there's no timeline padding for internal handoffs between teams.
