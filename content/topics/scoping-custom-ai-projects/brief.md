# Post Brief

**Title**: Why Most Custom AI Projects Never Ship — And How to Scope One That Will

**Target keyword**: how to scope a custom AI project

**Audience**: Founders and technical leads at small-to-mid-size companies (roughly 2–250 people — spans the "Startup" through "Medium Business" bands in our own People schema) who are evaluating whether to commission an AI agent, RAG system, knowledge graph, or custom LLM application. Many have already gotten, or are wary of getting, an agency proposal that reads like generic software SOW — fixed feature list, fixed timeline — for a kind of project that doesn't behave like generic software. This is the top of our funnel: the post should read well to someone who has never talked to us, not an existing lead.

**Angle**: Most "why AI projects fail" content blames the model, the data, or "AI hype." The real, more specific reason most custom AI builds never ship: they get scoped like ordinary software (features + timeline) instead of scoped like a probabilistic system, where the real open question is "does this use case actually fit this architecture" — and that is unknown until someone tests it, not something you can promise away in a sales deck. A fixed-scope AI engagement only works if the scoping phase treats use-case-to-architecture fit as the deliverable of a short, paid discovery step — not a free proposal call that leads straight into a fixed-price build. This is a direct, defensible expression of how Jasper AI actually works (use-case evaluation, architecture design, and implementation planning as a named, paid service before any fixed-scope build is quoted) — not a generic "AI is transformative" thought-leadership post. It also plays to the credibility of being a solo engineer: no handoffs between a sales team that scopes the deal and an engineering team that has to live with the scope.

**Hook idea**: Open with a specific, falsifiable claim rather than a stat that can't be sourced — something like: "Most custom AI engagements don't die because the model was wrong. They die because nobody could name the one decision the model actually had to get right, until it was too late to change the architecture." Frame it as a pattern seen across scoping conversations, not a cited statistic.

**Supporting points**:
- Ordinary software scoping (a fixed feature list + a timeline) works because the risk is mostly execution risk. AI project risk is different: the open question is whether the use case fits the chosen architecture at all — RAG vs. fine-tuning vs. a knowledge graph — and that's unknown until it's tested against real data, not something a sales proposal can respond for in advance.
- The fix: separate a short, explicitly paid discovery phase (use-case evaluation + architecture design) from the fixed-scope build itself, so the fixed price for the build comes *after* the biggest unknown has been tested, not before.
- Give the reader one concrete question to ask any AI vendor before signing: "What's the one architecture decision this project actually hinges on, and how will we know by week two if it's wrong?" — a vendor who can't answer this hasn't scoped it, they've priced it.
- Use the RAG vs. fine-tuning vs. knowledge graph contrast as a concrete illustration: three project types that all get pitched under the same "AI agent" label, and each fails in a completely different way if the architecture doesn't match the use case.
- Name why a solo AI engineer is structurally better positioned to make this call fast than an agency account team: the person scoping the discovery phase is the same person who owns the build, so there's no handoff where scope gets diluted between sales and engineering.
